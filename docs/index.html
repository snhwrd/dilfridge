01 Precooling

Bring the gas down to a few kelvin

A pulse-tube cryocooler first cools the two warmest cryogenic flanges. Copper links carry heat from the flanges to the cold head, and the flanges cool the radiation shields and intercept heat conducted down the wiring.

The incoming 3He-rich gas loses heat to these stages on its way down. Below the 4 K stage, the condensing circuit cools it further so it can enter the dilution unit as a liquid.

02 Counterflow

Use the returning fluid to precool the feed

Cold dilute solution rises from the mixing chamber. Concentrated 3He moves down in a separate channel and transfers heat to the colder return stream through the tube wall.

Many units add discrete exchangers with sintered metal at the cold end. Their large wetted surface helps overcome thermal resistance between the helium and the metal.

03 Dilution

Absorb heat across the phase boundary

Below about 0.87 K, a mixture of suitable composition separates into a lighter, 3He-rich phase above a denser, 4He-rich phase. Even near absolute zero, the dilute phase contains about 6.6 mol% 3He.

Each mole of 3He that enters the dilute phase carries away enthalpy. Continuous circulation maintains this transfer and removes heat from the chamber and its thermally coupled experiment.

At low temperature with the feed precooled to the chamber temperature, the ideal cooling power is approximately Q̇ ≈ 84 ṅ₃ T² in watts when T is in kelvin and the 3He molar flow ṅ₃ is in mol/s. Cooling power derivation

04 Recovery

Evaporate 3He and return it to the inlet

The still receives the dilute solution. Near 0.7 K, 3He has a much higher vapor pressure than 4He, so pumping above the liquid removes mostly 3He. A heater helps set the evaporation and circulation rate.

The room-temperature gas handling system pumps the vapor, cleans it and sends it back into the condensing line. Removing 3He from the still sustains the supply of dilute solution from the mixing chamber.
